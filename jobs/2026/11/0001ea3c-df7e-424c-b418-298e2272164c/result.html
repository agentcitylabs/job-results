PawPair — 10-Slide Pitch Deck Outline

Slide 1 — Title: "PawPair: Your dog's regular walker, right around the corner."

Key point: Name, tagline, and a one-line description: "PawPair matches dog owners with vetted local walkers for consistent, trackable walks." Founder name and contact at the bottom. This is the slide people photograph — make the tagline do the work.

Slide 2 — Problem: "65 million dogs. Not enough lunch breaks."

Key point: Dog owners are gone 8–10 hours a day, and dogs pay for it — pent-up energy, anxiety, chewed baseboards. Current options fail on trust: big marketplaces feel anonymous, and you rarely get the same walker twice. Anchor with one stat (≈65M US dog-owning households) and one human detail — the guilt of the 6 a.m. goodbye.

Slide 3 — Solution: "A vetted neighbor at the door, booked in under a minute."

Key point: PawPair matches owners with local walkers by location, schedule, and dog temperament — then keeps the same walker on repeat bookings. Every walker passes a background check, reference calls, and a supervised trial walk. One sentence to land: consistency for the dog, certainty for the owner.

Slide 4 — How It Works: "Three taps to a walked dog."

Key point: Show the flow, not a feature list. Owner side: build a dog profile → get matched → book recurring walks → watch it happen. Walker side: set availability, accept walks in their neighborhood, get paid weekly. Trust features run through everything: GPS-tracked routes, photo "report cards," in-app payment, and per-walk insurance.

Slide 5 — Market: "A walkable $1.4B market."

Key point: TAM/SAM/SOM funnel. TAM: ~$11B US pet services (walking, boarding, grooming). SAM: ~$1.4B urban dog walking. SOM: three launch cities in 24 months. Working estimates — verify against current APPA and market research figures before investor meetings.

Slide 6 — Business Model: "We take 18% of every wag."

Key point: Primary revenue is an 18% commission per walk (average walk price $22 → ~$4 to PawPair). Secondary: PawPair Plus, a $9.99/month owner subscription with priority booking and 10% off walks — it monetizes the desire for consistency. One clean unit-economics box: average owner books 3 walks/week → ~$200/year revenue per active owner on commission alone.

Slide 7 — Traction: "The dogs have already voted."

Key point: Lead with your strongest proof. Placeholder figures from a model pilot — replace with real data: six-week pilot in one neighborhood, 400+ walks completed, 4.9 average rating, 60% of owners rebooking by week four, 300-name waitlist. If pre-launch, use waitlist signups and walker applications instead. One metric, big font, is better than six small ones.

Slide 8 — Go-to-Market: "Win the block, then the city."

Key point: Density-first launch, neighborhood by neighborhood — never spread thin. Supply: recruit walkers from vet-tech programs, gig-worker communities, and retired neighbors. Demand: partnerships with apartment buildings and vet clinics, dog-park presence, and a referral program (a free walk for both sides). The block-by-block approach is also the moat — see slide 9.

Slide 9 — Competition: "Rover finds you a stranger. We find you a neighbor."

Key point: 2×2 grid or simple table vs. Rover, Wag, and informal options (the neighbor kid, Craigslist). Axes that favor you: walker consistency and local density. Differentiators: same-walker matching, deeper vetting, and pair-level ratings (we score each dog–walker pair, not just each walker). Acknowledge the giants directly — pretending Rover doesn't exist costs credibility.

Slide 10 — The Ask: "$750K to walk 10,000 dogs."

Key point: Raising $750K seed. Use of funds: 40% product, 35% walker acquisition and vetting, 25% launch marketing. 18-month milestones: 3 cities, 10,000 dogs on the platform, $1M ARR run rate. End with the tagline and contact — the same line as slide 1, so it's the first and last thing they hear.

Lean Canvas — PawPair (with notes)

1. Problem

Owners are away 8–10 hours daily; dogs are under-exercised and anxious; existing platforms deliver inconsistent, anonymous walkers, so trust breaks down.

Note: List existing alternatives honestly — Rover, Wag, dog daycare, favors from neighbors. Investors will compare you against "do nothing," too.

2. Customer Segments

Demand side: urban and inner-suburban dog owners, 28–55, household income $75K+, apartment dwellers or long commuters. Early adopters: young professionals in dense neighborhoods already fluent in gig apps. Supply side: vet-tech students, part-timers, active retirees.

Note: This is a two-sided market. The canvas centers the paying customer (owners); walkers show up under Channels and Cost Structure.

3. Unique Value Proposition

"Your dog's regular walker, booked in a tap — the same trusted neighbor, every time." High-level concept: Uber's ease, but with a familiar face.

Note: Consistency is the whole pitch. Every canvas block should bend toward it — if a feature doesn't serve consistency or trust, cut it.

4. Solution

Matching by location, schedule, temperament, and breed experience; recurring-schedule booking; vetted walker network; GPS tracking with photo report cards; in-app payment and per-walk insurance.

Note: Keep MVP scope tight — match, book, track. Insurance via a partner, not in-house. Everything else is a phase-two receipt.

5. Channels

Apartment-building partnerships, vet clinics and pet stores, dog-park and local-event presence, referral program (free walk both ways), local pet content on Instagram/TikTok.

Note: Track CAC per channel from day one of the pilot. Referral should become the cheapest channel at scale — if it isn't, the product isn't delighting anyone.

6. Revenue Streams

18% commission per walk (primary at launch); PawPair Plus subscription at $9.99/month; future streams: walker SaaS tools, pet-insurance referral fees.

Note: Commission proves the model; the subscription tests whether owners will pay extra for guaranteed consistency. Don't launch both on day one — sequence them so you can read the data.

7. Cost Structure

Background checks (~$25–40 per walker), per-walk insurance, payment processing (~3%), app development and hosting, per-city launch marketing, local support/ops.

Note: The biggest variable cost is trust — checks plus insurance. Do not trim it to improve margins. It's not overhead; it's the moat.

8. Key Metrics

Walks per active owner per week (target 3+); repeat booking rate at week 4 (target 60%+); match-to-first-walk conversion; 90-day walker retention; CAC vs. LTV per city; NPS.

Note: Repeat booking rate is the heartbeat metric. It directly measures whether the "regular walker" promise is real. Put it on slide 7 in the biggest font that fits.

9. Unfair Advantage

Neighborhood density lock-in — two-sided network effects at the block level; pair-level trust data (ratings for each dog–walker combination) that compounds over time; exclusive building partnerships.

Note: Density is the real moat. A competitor can clone the app in a quarter; they can't clone 40 vetted walkers on one neighborhood's doorsteps overnight.
