Discovery Call Script: Website Maintenance for Small Restaurants

Purpose: Qualify the prospect, uncover pain, and book the next step (proposal or follow-up demo). Tone: warm, no-jargon, respectful of their time — restaurant owners are busy between prep and service.

Opening (Goal: build rapport fast, state purpose, get permission)

"Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] from [Your Company]. Thanks for picking this up — I know between service and ordering, time's tight. I'll keep this to about 10 minutes.
The reason I'm calling: I help small restaurants like [Restaurant Name] make sure their website actually works for them — not just sits there looking nice but outdated. Before I pitch anything, I'd love to ask a few quick questions to see if this is even a fit. Sound okay?"

Wait for a yes. If they're rushed: "No problem — want me to call back at a better time, or do you have 5 minutes right now?"

Five Discovery Questions

1. "When's the last time someone updated your website — menu, hours, photos, anything?"

Why ask: Surfaces neglect. Most small restaurant sites are 2-3+ years stale. Their answer tells you urgency level without you having to say "your site looks old."

2. "Who handles that right now — you, someone on staff, an agency, or honestly... nobody?"

Why ask: Identifies decision-maker and current cost/effort. "Nobody" is your golden answer — it means no competing relationship to displace.

3. "Have customers ever mentioned something was wrong on the site — wrong hours, old menu prices, a broken reservation link?"

Why ask: This turns abstract neglect into a concrete, embarrassing business problem. Lets them tell you the pain in their own words, which you'll use later.

4. "If you wanted to update your holiday hours or add a new seasonal dish tonight, could you do it yourself, or would you have to call someone?"

Why ask: Exposes the friction and delay in their current setup. Sets up the value of ongoing maintenance versus a "set it and forget it" site.

5. "What's your website actually doing for you right now — bringing in reservations, orders, just existing for Google, or you're honestly not sure?"

Why ask: Gets at ROI expectations. "Not sure" is common and opens the door to talking about analytics/tracking as part of your maintenance package.

Listen more than you talk here. Take notes — you'll mirror their exact words back in the close.

Transition (Goal: reflect back what you heard, build the case)

"Okay, that's really helpful. So if I'm hearing you right — [restate 1-2 pain points in their words, e.g., 'your menu's been the same since spring and you're the one who has to find time to update it, which never happens']. That's exactly the kind of thing we take off your plate."

The Close (Goal: book next step, not close the full sale on this call)

"Here's what I'd suggest: let me put together a quick, no-pressure look at what maintenance would actually involve for a site like yours — things like keeping your hours and menu current, making sure it loads fast on phones, fixing anything broken, and a monthly check-in so nothing slips.
I can have that ready by [specific day — e.g., 'Thursday']. Would a 15-minute call then work to walk through it, or would you rather I just send it over by email first?"

Give them the choice — call or email — people engage more when they pick the format.

If they hesitate on price upfront:

"Totally fair to want numbers now — plans usually run between [$X–$Y] a month depending on how much hands-on work your site needs. I'll have exact pricing for your specific site in that follow-up."

If they say "we're not interested / don't have budget":

"Totally understand — can I ask, is it more that the site's not a priority right now, or that you've been burned by this kind of service before?"

(This single question often reopens the conversation — budget objections are frequently trust objections in disguise.)

Confirm and end:

"Great, I've got you down for [Thursday at 2pm / sending the email by Wednesday]. I'll also send a quick confirmation so it's on your radar. Thanks so much for the time, [Name] — good luck with service tonight!"

Quick Notes for the Caller

  • Never pitch the full service on this call. The goal is booking the next step, not closing.
  • Use their words, not yours, when summarizing pain — it proves you listened.
  • If the restaurant has no website at all, pivot Question 1 to: "Do you currently have a website, and if not, what are you using instead — just social media, Google listing?" Then adjust the close to "building + maintaining" rather than just maintenance.
  • Keep energy conversational, not scripted-sounding — these are five guiding questions, not an interrogation. Follow natural tangents if they reveal more pain.
