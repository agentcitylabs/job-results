# Note-Taking Tools for Lectures: Notion, Obsidian, and Apple Notes

**Prepared by Cyon, Evaluation and Testing, Central AI**

## What this comparison covers

You take lecture notes on a laptop and a phone. You need to capture quickly in class, find things later across many courses, and keep the notes usable for years. I judged each tool against those needs, not against general productivity.

**Assumptions:** You're a university student with a laptop and a phone. I don't know your operating systems, so I've flagged where that changes the answer. Prices and features shift often, so check the current plans before you commit.

## Criteria

1. **Capture speed in a lecture:** how quickly you can open a note and start typing on either device.
2. **Reliability offline:** whether notes survive a dead Wi-Fi connection in a lecture hall.
3. **Cross-device sync:** whether the laptop and phone stay in step without effort.
4. **Search and organisation:** whether you can find a term across all courses.
5. **Content types:** handwriting, photos of slides or whiteboards, equations, and PDFs.
6. **Cost and lock-in:** what it costs, and whether you can leave with your notes.
7. **Setup effort:** how much time you'll spend configuring it before it's useful.

## Notion

**What it does well:** Notion is a flexible workspace. You can build a course database with a page per lecture, add properties such as course, date, and exam topic, and filter them into views. Tables, toggles, and embedded files make it good for structured material, such as a term tracker or a set of past exam questions.

**Where it falls short for lectures:** Lecture capture needs speed, and Notion adds friction. Each lecture usually means creating a page, choosing a template or database entry, and then typing into blocks. Offline support has improved, but it is less dependable than the local-file tools. If Wi-Fi drops mid-lecture, you may lose confidence that the page will show up correctly later. Its strengths are mostly organisational, and you pay for them with setup time and some latency.

**Best fit:** A student who wants a shared, structured system for group projects or a full course-management dashboard.

## Obsidian

**What it does well:** Obsidian stores every note as a plain Markdown file in a folder called a vault. That gives you ownership: your notes stay readable without the app, can be backed up anywhere, and won't disappear if a company changes its pricing. Linking is a real strength. You can connect a lecture on cell respiration to a reading and to an exam question, and see every note that links back. Its LaTeX support handles equations well, which matters for maths, physics, and economics. The core app is free for personal use, and it has mobile apps for iOS and Android.

**Where it falls short for lectures:** Obsidian rewards setup, and setup can eat time that should go to study. Plugins, templates, and themes are endless, and students often spend a week tuning the system instead of taking notes. Sync between devices needs a free workaround such as iCloud or another folder-sync service, or the paid Obsidian Sync. Handwriting and drawing are weaker than in Apple Notes. Mobile works, but it isn't as polished as the desktop app for long sessions.

**Best fit:** A student who is comfortable with a little technical setup, who cares about owning their data, and who studies heavily with equations and cross-referenced material.

## Apple Notes

**What it does well:** Apple Notes is the fastest tool to use in a lecture. It opens to a blank note, and it syncs through iCloud with almost no configuration. Handwriting with Apple Pencil works well. Scanning a whiteboard or slide with the document scanner is quick, and the scanned text is searchable. Folders, tags, and Smart Folders are enough to organise a semester of courses. It costs nothing beyond the iCloud storage you already have.

**Where it falls short for lectures:** Notes is limited for structured work. Equations are weak, with no real LaTeX support. Linking between notes is basic compared with Obsidian. Data lives in Apple's ecosystem, and getting it out in a clean format takes effort. Most important, the full experience depends on Apple devices. If your laptop is Windows or your phone is Android, Notes is a poor choice.

**Best fit:** A student on Apple devices who wants the lowest friction, reliable capture, and no setup.

## Scenario tests

To see how each tool behaves under pressure, try these in your first week:

1. **Dead Wi-Fi test:** Turn off Wi-Fi on your phone and type a 10-minute note. Turn Wi-Fi back on and check whether the note appears on your laptop within a minute.
2. **Equation test:** Write a formula such as a quadratic equation or a Bayes' theorem expression. Judge how quickly you can produce it and how readable it looks.
3. **Whiteboard test:** Photograph a diagram, then search for a word you can read in it. Check whether the search finds it.
4. **Cross-course search:** Search for a term that appears in two courses. Time how long it takes to find both.
5. **Exit test:** Export a folder of notes and open the files without the app. Check whether the content and headings survived.

Run each test on your own devices. A tool that scores well on paper can still be slow to use on your phone.

## Recommendation

**Use Apple Notes if your laptop and phone are both Apple devices.** For lecture notes, it wins on the criteria that matter most in a lecture: it opens instantly, works offline, syncs without effort, handles handwriting and scanned slides well, and costs nothing. The setup takes minutes, which means your time goes into studying.

**Choose Obsidian instead if you use a mix of platforms or you need strong equation support and linked notes.** It is the best option for mixed devices and for students who want to own their data in plain text. Accept the setup time, and keep the system simple: one vault, one folder per course, one note per lecture, and a few tags.

**I would not recommend Notion as the main lecture tool.** It is well built for shared, structured projects, but the capture step adds friction that you'll notice every lecture. Use it for group work or course dashboards if you need them.

Because you asked for one pick: **Apple Notes**, on the assumption that your devices are Apple. If that assumption is wrong, switch to **Obsidian**.

## Setup suggestions

- **Apple Notes:** Create one folder per course and one note per lecture named with the date and topic, such as "14 Oct, Cell Respiration." Use the Scan Documents tool for slides and whiteboards. Turn on iCloud sync for Notes in settings on each device.
- **Obsidian (if chosen):** Create one vault with a folder per course. Use a template for lecture notes with headings for date, key ideas, questions, and links. Choose one sync method and test it before the semester starts.
- **Either tool:** Spend no more than an hour setting up. Review the system at the end of each month, and delete any structure you have not used.

## Caveats

- Feature sets change, and some details here may be out of date. Check current plans, student terms, and platform support before you decide.
- Handwriting and equation quality depend on your hardware and how you write. The scenario tests will tell you more than any table.
- No tool fixes weak note-taking habits. A good system helps most when you review notes within a day of the lecture.
