# Two 15-Second Ad Scripts — Language-Learning App

**Prepared by Pixel · Creative coder for generative visuals**

**Working assumptions** (so nothing stalls): the app is referred to as **[APP]** — drop in the real name, syllable count is flexible. I'm assuming a freemium model with a short daily-lesson format and speaking practice, and that these run as vertical 9:16 paid social spots with sound-on primary, captions burned in. Both scripts are built to 15.0 seconds at a natural read speed, with the last 2.5 seconds reserved for the end card so you can swap CTAs per market without re-editing. Both are live-action-light: one actor, found locations, plus a generative motion layer I can produce in-house.

## Version A — "The Sentence You Didn't Say"

**Hook type:** confession / recognition. Opens inside a specific social failure, not a product.

| Time | Visual | Audio |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0–2.5 | Tight on a woman, late 20s, at a bakery counter in Lisbon. Her mouth opens. Nothing comes out. She points. Freeze frame on the point. | **VO (her, internal, close-mic):** "I've studied Portuguese for two years. And I just pointed at a croissant." |
| 2.5–5.0 | Freeze holds; a thin generative line-drawing blooms out of her pointing finger — the sentence she *meant* to say, written in light, curling across the shop. | Soft synth swell. Pastry-shop ambience ducks under. |
| 5.0–8.0 | Cut to her at home, phone in hand, [APP] open. She's speaking into it. The waveform on screen blooms into the same line-drawing language, now steady. | **VO:** "So I stopped memorizing. I started *talking*." |
| 8.0–11.0 | Fast triptych: morning bus, speaking; kitchen, correcting herself, laughing; late evening, lamp on, mouthing a phrase. Generative ribbons trail each cut. | **VO:** "Five minutes a day. Out loud. Even when it's wrong." |
| 11.0–12.5 | Return to the bakery. Same counter, different day. She speaks — we hear the real line, clean, unsubtitled for half a beat, then captioned. | **Her (on camera, in Portuguese):** "Um pastel de nata, por favor. E… o senhor tem um bom dia." Baker laughs. |
| 12.5–15.0 | End card. Logo resolves out of the same line-drawing system. | **VO:** "[APP]. Say it before you're ready." |

**On-screen text:**
- 0.5s: `pointed at a croissant` (small, lower third)
- 12.6s: `Say it before you're ready.`
- 13.8s: `Start free — [APP]`

**Audience this suits:** Lapsed and anxious learners — people who already *have* vocabulary and are stuck on the speaking cliff. Typically 25–40, have done a streak-based app or a semester of classes, can read a menu but freeze at a counter. The hook works because it's not aspirational, it's *embarrassing*, and embarrassment is the most shareable emotion in this category. Strongest on Instagram Reels and TikTok to travel-adjacent and expat interest clusters, and as a retargeting creative for users who installed, lapsed, and never completed a speaking lesson.

**Why it holds the scroll:** the first line names a failure specific enough to be true. "Pointed at a croissant" is a detail no stock script would write, and detail is what buys you second three.

## Version B — "4 Minutes, 11 Seconds"

**Hook type:** number / found-time. Opens with a hard stat about the viewer's own day.

| Time | Visual | Audio |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0–2.0 | Black. A single number types on in monospace: `4:11`. Beneath it, smaller: `average time you spend waiting for coffee.` | **VO (dry, warm, male or female):** "Four minutes, eleven seconds." |
| 2.0–4.0 | Snap cut: a man, 30s, in a café queue, phone up, headphones in, lips moving. Overhead shot. The queue shuffles forward around him. | **VO:** "That's how long you stand in line for coffee." |
| 4.0–7.0 | The timer reappears as a generative column of particles beside him, draining downward. Each particle that falls becomes a word he says, stacking into a sentence on screen. | **VO:** "It's also one [APP] lesson. Spoken, not swiped." |
| 7.0–10.0 | Cut series, each locked to a beat: train platform, 1:50 on screen. Lift lobby, 0:40. Microwave, 1:05. Different people, same gesture — talking quietly into a phone. | **VO:** "The platform. The lift. Waiting on the microwave." |
| 10.0–12.0 | A progress ring closes. A gentle chime. His name on a streak card: `Day 64`. He looks up — he's at the front of the queue. | **VO:** "Sixty-four days. Zero extra hours." |
| 12.0–15.0 | He orders — in Japanese, in a Tokyo café, which recontextualizes the whole spot. End card resolves from the particle column. | **Him:** "Hot, no sugar." *(subtitled)* **VO:** "[APP]. Learn in the gaps." |

**On-screen text:**
- 0.2s: `4:11` then `average coffee queue`
- 7.2s: `one lesson = one queue`
- 12.4s: `Learn in the gaps.`
- 13.6s: `Free to start — [APP]`

**Audience this suits:** Time-poor working adults who have *decided* they want a language and keep failing on schedule, not motivation. 28–45, commuters, parents, people with calendars that are already full and who respond to efficiency framing rather than wanderlust framing. The number hook is a pattern-interrupt that reads as information rather than advertising, which is why it survives YouTube pre-roll and Facebook feed — environments where an emotional cold open gets skipped at second four but a concrete figure buys a beat of curiosity. Also the better of the two for B2B-adjacent placements (LinkedIn, professional-development contexts) and for cold acquisition where you have no prior relationship with the viewer.

**Why it holds the scroll:** it asks nothing and gives something. The viewer learns a fact about their own life in the first two seconds, and the Tokyo reveal at 12s pays off the whole thing retroactively, which drives rewatch — your single most useful metric on short vertical.

## Production notes for both

**The shared visual language.** I'd build one generative system and let both spots inherit it: a particle-and-line engine where spoken words become visible structure. In A it blooms organically — vines, handwriting, breath. In B the same engine runs on a grid, mechanical and countable. Same code, two moods. That keeps the campaign coherent even though the hooks pull in opposite directions, and it means the end-card logo resolve is a reusable 2.5-second asset across every future cut.

**Casting.** Both spots need actors with real functional ability in the target language, or at minimum a convincing accent coached on-site. Nothing kills a language ad faster than a hero line delivered badly — the exact audience you're courting is the audience that can hear it.

**Sound.** Script A should feel close and slightly muffled, like the inside of your own head, until the bakery line snaps into open room tone. Script B is dry, tight, metronomic, with the chime at 11.5s as the only warm sound in the piece. Mix both for sound-off survival anyway: captions carry the full meaning in each.

**Localisation.** Swap the target language per market — Portuguese and Japanese are placeholders. A's hook survives translation because the croissant-point is universal. B's hook needs its number re-sourced per market, since queue times differ; I'd use a locally plausible figure rather than a global average.

**Testing.** Run them as a straight two-cell split on identical audiences before you assume the targeting split above is correct. My bet: A wins on share rate and comment volume, B wins on install-per-impression and day-7 retention, because it pre-sells a realistic habit. If that's what you see, the move is A for top-of-funnel awareness and B for the conversion-weighted spend — not one winner.

**Cutdowns available from this material at no extra shoot:** a 6-second bumper from A (0.0–2.5 plus end card, "pointed at a croissant" as the entire idea) and a 6-second from B (the `4:11` type-on straight to the Tokyo order).
