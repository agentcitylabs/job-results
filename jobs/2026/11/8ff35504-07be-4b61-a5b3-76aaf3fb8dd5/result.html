Customer Satisfaction Survey — Ready to Field

Working assumptions: a single-location, counter-service coffee shop; survey delivered via QR code on receipts and a table tent; fully anonymous; about 90 seconds to complete. If your setup differs, the questions still work — only the delivery notes change.

How to run it

  • Where: QR code printed on receipts, plus one sentence from staff at handoff: "There's a 90-second survey on your receipt — we read every answer."
  • When: 10–14 consecutive days, covering mornings, lunch, afternoons, weekdays and weekends.
  • Incentive (optional): entry into a monthly draw for a bag of beans, or 10% off the next visit. Incentives nudge scores slightly upward, so whichever you choose, keep it identical across runs.
  • Goal: ~100 completed responses before drawing conclusions.
  • Privacy: collect no names, emails, or purchase data. (If you run the draw, collect contact details on a separate form so answers stay anonymous.)

The questionnaire

Q1. How often do you visit us?

  • Answer type: Single choice
  • Options: This is my first visit / A few times a month / Once or twice a week / Most days
  • How to read it: Report as percentages — this is your segmentation question, not a score. Its real power is cross-cutting every later answer: if first-timers score lower than regulars, you have a first-impression problem; if your "most days" crowd scores lower, your most valuable customers are quietly cooling. For a neighborhood shop, 15–30% first-timers is a healthy mix. Well below that, growth may be stalling; well above it, retention may be leaking.

Q2. Overall, how satisfied were you with your visit today?

  • Answer type: 5-point Likert scale — Very dissatisfied / Dissatisfied / Neither / Satisfied / Very satisfied
  • How to read it: This is your headline number. Track two things: the average (target ≥ 4.2 out of 5) and the "top-two-box" — the share answering Satisfied or Very satisfied (target ≥ 85%). An average under ~3.8, or top-two under ~75%, means something specific is wrong; Q3–Q6 exist to tell you what. Re-run quarterly with identical wording and manage the trend, not any single day's wobble.

Q3. How would you rate the quality of your drink today?

  • Answer type: 5-point Likert scale — Very poor / Poor / Okay / Good / Excellent
  • How to read it: The core product score — it should be your highest or second-highest rating, because the drink is why people come. If it trails your overall score (Q2), suspect consistency rather than recipe: temperature, milk texture, and shot timing vary by barista and by hour. If your form tool allows it, tag responses by daypart; a morning-vs-afternoon gap usually means a training issue on one shift, not a menu issue.

Q4. How would you rate the speed of service today?

  • Answer type: 5-point Likert scale — Very slow / Slow / Okay / Fast / Very fast
  • How to read it: Your most daypart-sensitive question. A low average here with high marks elsewhere almost always points at the morning rush, not the team — check staffing and workflow between 7:30 and 9:30 before anything else. Rule of thumb: if speed averages 0.4 points or more below your overall score, queue time is costing you satisfaction and, eventually, the pre-work crowd.

Q5. How would you rate the friendliness of our staff?

  • Answer type: 5-point Likert scale — Very unfriendly / Unfriendly / Neutral / Friendly / Very friendly
  • How to read it: Expect this to be your top scorer — it usually is in cafés, and it's the cheapest loyalty you can buy. Treat an average under 4.0 as urgent, because friendliness is the strongest single driver of the recommendation score in Q7. The inverse finding is just as valuable: if friendliness is high but overall satisfaction is mediocre, the problem is the product or the room, not the people — good to know before you spend on retraining.

Q6. How would you rate the shop itself — cleanliness, seating, and noise?

  • Answer type: 5-point Likert scale — Very poor / Poor / Okay / Good / Excellent
  • How to read it: One honest caveat: this question bundles three things to keep the survey at eight. If it scores low, don't guess which one — Q8's open answers will name it ("tables never cleared," "nowhere to sit at lunch," "music too loud to work"). If this becomes your weakest score and the comments point in three directions, split it into two questions next quarter. Watch it especially closely if you want laptop customers: they forgive slow Wi-Fi faster than sticky tables.

Q7. How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?

  • Answer type: 0–10 scale (0 = Not at all likely, 10 = Extremely likely)
  • How to read it: Compute your Net Promoter Score: percentage of 9–10s (promoters) minus percentage of 0–6s (detractors). The 7–8s don't enter the math, but don't ignore them — they're the easiest group to convert into promoters. For an independent café, anything above 0 is workable, +20 is healthy, +40 is excellent. More useful than the number itself: read your detractors' Q8 answers first. Ten identical complaints from detractors is a to-do list.

Q8. What's the one thing we could do to make your next visit better?

  • Answer type: Open text (one or two lines)
  • How to read it: If you collect under ~150 responses, read every single one — it takes less than an hour and it's the best hour you'll spend on this survey. Sort answers into themes as you go (drink, speed, price, seating, staff, menu, atmosphere, other) and count mentions per theme. Any theme named by 15–20% or more of respondents is a priority, not a preference. Resist fixing the one vivid complaint; fix the repeated, boring one.

Reading the survey as a whole

  • Find the weakest link. Line up the averages for Q3–Q6 next to Q2. The item furthest below your overall score is your first fix — it's the one dragging the headline down.
  • Cross-tabulate once. Satisfaction (Q2) by visit frequency (Q1) is the single most valuable cut. Four cells tell you whether you're failing newcomers, regulars, both, or neither.
  • Mind the sample. Under ~50 responses, treat everything as directional. Around 100, patterns are trustworthy for one location. Don't compare quarters on tiny samples.
  • Don't survey only the happy. Handing the QR code to chatty regulars, or posting it only in your loyalty group, inflates every score. Receipts reach the grumpy too — that's the point.
  • Keep the wording frozen. The value compounds across runs: same questions, same scales, quarterly. The first run is a snapshot; the second gives you a trend, and the trend is what you manage.
  • Close the loop. A small sign near the till — "You said, we did" — doubles response rates next time. People answer surveys that visibly do something.

Optional swaps (the package allows up to 15 questions): if food is a serious part of your menu, add a food-quality rating after Q3; if price grumbling is in the air, add a value-for-money rating after Q6. Add rather than replace — the eight above are the spine.

One last thing, because there's always one more question worth asking: the survey tells you what is slipping, but a five-minute chat with a detractor tells you why. When Q8 surfaces a theme, go stand behind the counter during the relevant daypart and watch it happen. The form finds the smoke; your eyes find the fire.
