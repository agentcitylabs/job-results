## Summary

This comparison evaluates Notion, Obsidian, and Apple Notes for a student taking lecture notes on both a laptop and a phone.  
It covers note-taking, organization, syncing, offline use, collaboration, privacy, and cost.  
**Recommendation: Notion** for most students because it offers the best balance of cross-device access, organization, search, and ease of use.

## At-a-glance comparison

| Tool | Best for | Main strengths | Main drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Notion** | Students who want an organized all-in-one workspace | Flexible pages, databases, templates, web access, collaboration | Offline use is less dependable; can be more complex than necessary |
| **Obsidian** | Students who value local files, linking, and long-term control | Excellent Markdown support, powerful links, offline-first workflow | Sync and collaboration may require extra setup or payment |
| **Apple Notes** | Students fully invested in Apple devices | Very quick, reliable syncing, handwriting and attachments, simple interface | Limited outside the Apple ecosystem; less powerful for complex organization |

## Notion

### Lecture-note workflow

Notion works well for creating a page for each course, with subpages for individual lectures. A student can add headings, checklists, images, PDFs, tables, and links. Templates can standardize every lecture note—for example:

- Date and lecture topic
- Key concepts
- Definitions
- Questions to review
- Follow-up reading
- Exam relevance

Notion databases can also track assignments, reading lists, deadlines, and revision topics alongside lecture notes.

### Laptop and phone experience

Notion has apps for major desktop and mobile platforms, as well as a web version. This makes it practical when a student moves between a laptop, phone, Windows, macOS, iPhone, or Android device. Notes are generally easy to find through search and navigation.

The mobile app is suitable for reviewing notes and making short additions, though writing long notes is more comfortable on a laptop.

### Limitations

Notion depends more heavily on an internet connection than the other two options. Offline access exists, but it is not the tool’s strongest feature and should be tested before relying on it during a lecture. Very large or highly elaborate workspaces can also feel slower or more complicated than a simple note app.

## Obsidian

### Lecture-note workflow

Obsidian stores notes as Markdown files in a local folder. This makes it excellent for students who want ownership of their files and the ability to keep using them outside the app.

Its strongest feature is linking. A student can link a lecture note to related concepts, readings, people, or assignments. Over time, this can create a useful connected knowledge base rather than a collection of isolated documents.

Obsidian also supports:

- Backlinks between related notes
- Tags and folders
- Templates
- Community extensions
- Graph views of connected topics
- Markdown export and portability

### Laptop and phone experience

Obsidian is available on desktop and mobile platforms. Because notes are local files, they remain available offline, which is valuable in lecture halls with poor connectivity.

The main complication is synchronizing those files across devices. Obsidian offers its own sync service, while other methods—such as cloud storage—can require technical setup and may not work equally well on every platform. A student should choose one reliable sync method rather than combining several.

### Limitations

Obsidian has a steeper learning curve. Its flexibility is useful, but students may spend too much time designing folders, tags, plugins, and workflows instead of studying. Real-time collaboration is also not its main strength. It is best for an individual building a durable personal knowledge system.

## Apple Notes

### Lecture-note workflow

Apple Notes is the fastest option to start using. A student can create folders for courses, pin important notes, scan handouts, add photos, draw diagrams, and attach files. Search is particularly convenient for finding text in typed notes and, in supported cases, scanned or handwritten content.

It is a good fit for straightforward lecture capture, especially when the student wants minimal setup.

### Laptop and phone experience

Apple Notes syncs smoothly through iCloud between an iPhone and Mac. The interface is consistent, and notes are easy to open quickly on a phone. It also works well with Apple features such as document scanning, handwriting, audio-related workflows, and sharing.

The main limitation appears when the student uses a Windows laptop, Android phone, or other non-Apple device. Web access exists, but the experience is generally less integrated than using Apple Notes within the Apple ecosystem.

### Limitations

Apple Notes has fewer tools for building a structured academic knowledge base. It supports folders, tags, links, and smart folders, but it is less flexible than Notion for databases and less powerful than Obsidian for linked, long-term research notes.

Collaboration is available, but it is not the central strength of the app. Students who need shared project workspaces may prefer Notion.

## Comparison by student need

| Need | Best choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Fastest setup | Apple Notes | Minimal configuration and familiar interface |
| Works across laptop and phone brands | Notion | Strong app and web availability |
| Reliable offline access | Obsidian | Notes are stored locally |
| Building links between ideas | Obsidian | Backlinks and connected Markdown notes |
| Course dashboards and assignment tracking | Notion | Databases and templates |
| Apple-only study setup | Apple Notes | Smoothest iPhone–Mac integration |
| Group projects and shared notes | Notion | Collaboration is built into the workspace |
| Maximum file ownership and portability | Obsidian | Plain Markdown files remain usable independently |

## Recommendation: Notion

I recommend **Notion** for the typical student in this situation.

It provides the most balanced experience for taking notes on a laptop and reviewing them on a phone. A student can create one workspace for school, separate courses into pages, use a consistent lecture template, and add an assignment or revision database without needing to manage files or technical synchronization.

Notion is especially suitable when:

- The student may use different operating systems.
- Notes need to be accessible through a browser.
- Courses include assignments, reading lists, and project planning.
- The student wants to share notes or collaborate with classmates.
- A structured workspace is more useful than a plain list of documents.

A practical setup would be:

1. Create a top-level **School** page.
2. Create one page per course.
3. Add a lecture-note template with sections for summary, key terms, questions, and revision tasks.
4. Keep a separate database for assignments and exams.
5. Test offline access before using Notion during an important lecture.
6. Export or back up especially important material periodically.

### When to choose another option

Choose **Apple Notes** instead if the student uses only Apple devices and wants the simplest, fastest, most dependable everyday note-taking experience.

Choose **Obsidian** instead if offline access, local file ownership, Markdown portability, and links between ideas matter more than convenience and collaboration.

## Notes

- This recommendation assumes the student’s device ecosystem is not known, so cross-platform access is weighted heavily.
- Pricing, storage limits, offline behavior, and feature availability can change by plan, operating system, or app version; the student should verify current details before subscribing.
- The student should test synchronization and offline access with a small sample workspace before making Notion the sole location for essential academic notes.
- For any tool, a separate backup of important notes is sensible, especially before graduation or account changes.
