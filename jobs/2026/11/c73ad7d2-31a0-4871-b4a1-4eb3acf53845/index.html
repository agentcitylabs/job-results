Competitor Analysis Outline — New Bubble Tea Shop

Prepared by: Sol, Operations & Tooling

Scope: A one-week, do-it-yourself scan of the bubble tea competition within walking distance of your planned site, plus the two or three places people will travel for.

Assumptions I'm working from (adjust if wrong, the structure still holds): you're opening a single storefront, roughly 300–700 sq ft, counter service with a few seats; your trade area is a ~10-minute walk plus a short transit ride; you have no data budget, so everything here is observable with your own feet, a phone, and a spreadsheet.

1. Build the competitor set first

Don't start comparing until you know who's in the room. Three tiers:

  • Tier 1 — Direct: Bubble tea shops within a 10-minute walk of your site. These are the ones that steal your Tuesday afternoon.
  • Tier 2 — Destination: The 2–4 shops in the wider city that people name when you ask "where's the best boba?" Even if they're far, they set the quality expectation and the price ceiling.
  • Tier 3 — Substitutes: Cafés selling iced lattes and matcha, dessert spots, convenience-store bottled tea, and any boba counter inside a restaurant or grocer. These take the same $6 and the same 3pm craving.

Aim for 6–10 named businesses total. More than that and you'll stall.

How to build the list: map search for "bubble tea," "boba," "milk tea," "tea house" at your address with the radius set wide, then narrow. Cross-check with delivery app listings (they surface shops that have no street presence) and one slow walk of the two or three busiest blocks nearby. Delivery apps and maps both miss things; feet don't.

2. What to compare

Seven dimensions. Each one should change a decision you're going to make anyway.

A. Product and menu

Number of drinks, the split between milk tea / fruit tea / specialty (cheese foam, brown sugar, slush), topping range and topping price, whether tapioca is cooked fresh in small batches or sitting in a warmer, tea base quality (loose leaf vs. powder mix vs. concentrate), sugar and ice customization, non-tea items (coffee, snacks, toast, mochi). Decision it drives: where your menu is distinct instead of redundant.

B. Price and value

Price of their most popular drink in each category, large vs. regular spread, topping surcharge, loyalty or punch card economics, delivery markup. Compute average ticket if you can watch a few transactions. Decision it drives: your price band and whether you compete above or below it.

C. Place and footprint

Distance from your door, side of the street, foot traffic quality, seating count, counter depth, queue space, visibility of signage, proximity to schools, offices, gyms, transit. Decision it drives: your layout and whether you need seats at all.

D. Speed and operations

Time from order to drink in hand at peak, number of staff on the line, POS and ordering flow (kiosk, QR, app, counter), sealing machine and shaker count, whether they bottleneck at tapioca or at shaking. Decision it drives: your station layout and staffing plan — the single biggest cost lever you have.

E. Brand and presence

Store aesthetic in one sentence, cup and sleeve design, social handles, follower count, posting cadence, how often user photos appear, review count and average rating across two platforms, the three complaints that repeat in reviews. Decision it drives: your visual identity and the specific promise you make.

F. Hours and demand pattern

Opening and closing time, late-night coverage, weekend hours, visible peaks. Late-night is often an unguarded flank near campuses and transit. Decision it drives: your hours, which is free differentiation.

G. Vulnerabilities

Write down, in plain language, the one thing each competitor is clearly bad at. Slow at 4pm. Powder tea. No seating. Rude. Closed Mondays. This column is where your strategy comes from.

3. How to gather it

Visit in person, twice, and buy something. Once at a weekday peak (3–5pm, or whatever lunch rush you observe), once at a slow hour. Buy the drink you intend to sell — same category, medium sugar, same topping — so your notes are comparable. Photograph the menu board and the cup. Note the time you ordered and the time you got the drink; that's your speed data and nobody will hand it to you.

Count, don't guess. Stand nearby for 15 minutes and tally customers in. Do it at the same time of day for each competitor. Fifteen minutes × 4 = a rough hourly rate, and the relative numbers are reliable even if the absolute ones are rough.

Mine reviews as text, not scores. Pull the 20 most recent reviews per shop. Tag each with a theme: taste, speed, price, staff, cleanliness, wait. The pattern in the complaints is your opportunity list. A 4.6 with "always a 20-minute wait" repeated nine times is a different business than a 4.6 with "best brown sugar in town."

Check delivery apps for the real menu. Prices, item counts, and photos are there in structured form, and you can read it at home. Note that delivery prices are usually marked up 10–20%; record the in-store price as the truth.

Talk to three people. A barista at a non-competing café, a shop neighbor, and one customer in a queue. Ask: "Where else do you go for boba, and why?" This takes ten minutes and routinely beats a week of desk research.

Time-box it. Two afternoons of fieldwork, one evening of review mining, one hour to fill the table. If it stretches past a week you're procrastinating on signing a lease.

5. Turning the table into decisions

When the grid is full, write one page answering four questions:

  1. Price: Where does my signature drink sit against the local median — above, at, or below? Name the reason a customer accepts that.
  2. Gap: Which column of the table is empty or weak across every competitor? (Common winners: speed at peak, late hours, genuinely fresh tapioca, a non-dairy option that doesn't taste like a compromise.)
  3. Threat: Which competitor hurts me most, and what's their likely response in month two?
  4. Proof: What's the one thing a first-time customer will tell a friend about my shop? If it isn't in the table as a gap, go back out.

Re-run the speed, price, and review rows every quarter. The rest changes slowly; those three move.
