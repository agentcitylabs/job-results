Customer Persona: Online Pet Supplies Store

Meet Maya Dolan — "The Devoted Planner"

Age: 34

Location: Suburban homeowner, 25 minutes from the nearest big-box pet store

Household: Married, one kid (age 7), two dogs (a senior Lab mix and a younger rescue terrier), one indoor cat

Occupation: Marketing coordinator, works hybrid (3 days remote)

Income: $68,000 household income, comfortable but budget-conscious

Assumption Note

Since the brief doesn't specify a target segment, I'm building around the most common high-value buyer for online pet supply stores: a multi-pet household owner who treats pets as family, shops online for convenience and selection, but still weighs price carefully. This persona is a composite drawn from typical pet-owner behavior patterns, not a single real customer.

Who She Is

Maya grew up with dogs and has never known a home without an animal in it. Her pets aren't an accessory to her life — they're part of the family structure, with names that show up in her holiday card and a shared Google calendar event for the Lab's arthritis meds. She's busy, slightly overextended, and treats pet care the way she treats everything else: with a running mental checklist she's trying to get off her brain and onto autopilot.

She's not a luxury shopper. She clips coupons for her own groceries. But she will spend more on her senior dog's joint supplement than she will on her own vitamins, because she feels guilty if she cuts corners there.

Goals

  • Keep three very different animals healthy without becoming a part-time vet tech. The senior Lab needs joint support and a sensitive-stomach diet; the terrier is food-motivated and overweight-prone; the cat is finicky and switches preferences every few months.
  • Never run out of something critical. Flea/tick prevention, prescription food, and litter are the things that cause actual panic if she's down to the last scoop.
  • Save time. She resents the 40-minute round trip to a specialty store just to get one bag of limited-ingredient kibble.
  • Feel like a good pet parent without overspending. She wants quality, but she also wants to know she's not being upsold on things that don't matter.
  • Find products that fit specific constraints — grain-free, weight management, senior formula, breed-size-specific — without having to read six reviews just to confirm a product won't upset someone's stomach.

Frustrations

  • Subscription traps. She's been burned before by "convenient" auto-ship programs that were hard to pause or cancel, or that shipped the wrong size bag after a dog's diet changed.
  • Inconsistent stock. Her senior dog's exact prescription-adjacent food was discontinued once with zero warning, and she had to scramble across three sites to find a replacement during a stressful week.
  • Generic marketing that ignores her actual problem. Ads that say "Treat your pet like family!" without addressing which pet, which issue, which life stage — she scrolls past all of it.
  • Decision fatigue from too many near-identical SKUs. She doesn't want 40 flavors of the same formula; she wants the three that fit her situation, clearly labeled.
  • **Shipping delays on things she needs now.** A late box of flea prevention during tick season is not a minor inconvenience to her — it's a trust breaker.
  • Customer service that feels like a chatbot maze when she has a real question, like whether a treat is safe alongside a specific medication.

Buying Habits

  • Researches once, then wants to go on autopilot. She'll spend real time the first time picking a food or supplement — reading ingredient lists, checking vet forums — but afterward she wants reordering to be nearly invisible.
  • Subscribes selectively. She'll set up auto-ship for consumables she's already vetted and trusts (litter, a specific kibble), but she manually buys anything new to "test" it before committing.
  • Shops mostly on her phone, mostly in the evening after her kid is in bed — this is when she remembers "oh, we're almost out of X."
  • Price-compares for commodity items (litter, basic treats) but is less price-sensitive on anything tied to health (supplements, prescription diets, dental care).
  • Reads reviews, especially ones that mention her specific situation — "my senior Lab with hip issues" or "my picky cat" — more than star ratings alone.
  • Responds to bundle logic, not generic discounts. A "senior dog starter kit" or "multi-pet household box" appeals more than "10% off sitewide," because it saves her the work of assembling the right combination herself.
  • Checks return/exchange policy before buying anything new for the cat specifically, because cats are unpredictable and she's wasted money before on food the cat sniffed once and rejected forever.

The Message That Wins Her

Maya doesn't need to be sold on loving her pets — she needs to be sold on reducing the mental load of caring for several different animals with different needs, without feeling like she's settling or overpaying.

Winning message framework:

"Three pets, three sets of needs, one less thing to think about."

This works because it:

  1. Names her actual situation (multi-pet, multi-need) instead of a generic pet-love platitude.
  2. Promises reduced cognitive load, which is her real currency — not just savings.
  3. Implies reliability without having to state it outright (the "one less thing" framing suggests she won't run out, won't get surprised, won't have to re-research).

Supporting proof points that would close her:

  • Flexible subscriptions she can edit in two taps, with visible "pause anytime" language front and center — not buried in account settings.
  • A clear, filterable way to shop by pet profile (she'd love being able to set up each animal once — age, size, condition — and get recommendations that respect all three without cross-contamination).
  • Transparent stock/restock timing on the specific items she depends on, especially anything prescription-adjacent.
  • Human customer support for edge-case questions (medication interactions, sizing for an in-between dog), not just an FAQ bot.
  • Bundles explicitly built around her kind of household ("multi-pet," "senior + young dog," "picky eater kit") rather than generic seasonal sales.

The line that would actually stop her scroll:

"Set it up once for all three pets. We'll remember what each one needs — so you don't have to."

That's the emotional register she buys from: not excitement, not luxury — relief.
