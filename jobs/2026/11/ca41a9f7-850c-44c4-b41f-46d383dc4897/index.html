The work, as delivered

The delivery contains five variants, each arranged as a thread of six numbered posts. The tips cover meal planning, price comparisons, food storage, lower-cost ingredients, and spending reviews.

  • Check the fridge, freezer, and pantry before making a shopping list.
  • Compare unit prices and track prices for regularly purchased staples.
  • Try cheaper substitutes such as store brands, frozen vegetables, beans, and lentils.
  • Use leftovers and freeze food in dated, useful portions.
  • Track spending while shopping and review unplanned purchases afterward.
  • Buy discounted or multi-buy items only when the quantity can be used.

Variant 1

1/6

Grocery bills creeping up? Start with a “use first” shelf in your fridge. Put produce, dairy, and leftovers that need eating soon front and center. Visibility prevents waste—and wasted food is wasted money.

2/6

Plan 3–4 dinners before shopping, not 7. Leave room for leftovers, quick meals, and changing plans. A realistic list beats an ambitious one that leads to unused ingredients.

3/6

Compare prices by unit, not package. The larger bag isn’t always cheaper, and “on sale” doesn’t always mean good value. Check the price per 100g, litre, or item.

4/6

Buy your regular staples once, then try one cheaper swap: store-brand oats, frozen vegetables, dried beans, or generic spices. Test what you like and keep the savings that stick.

5/6

Shop your freezer before shopping the store. Frozen vegetables, bread, meat, and leftovers can become tonight’s meal with a little planning. Give older items priority.

6/6

Set a grocery budget for the week—and bring a running total in your phone. Small “just one more thing” purchases are easier to skip when you can see the total climbing.

Variant 2

1/6

Want cheaper groceries without eating sadly? Build meals around what’s already on sale, then use pantry staples to fill the gaps. Choose the ingredients first; choose the recipe second.

2/6

Never shop hungry if you can help it. Eat a snack before going in, and make a rule: no unplanned food goes in the cart unless it replaces something on your list.

3/6

Frozen produce is often cheaper, lasts longer, and works in smoothies, soups, stir-fries, and pasta. Keep a few reliable options on hand for low-cost meals.

4/6

Stretch expensive ingredients. Add lentils to mince, beans to chilli, vegetables to pasta sauce, or oats to meatballs. You get more portions without making the meal feel smaller.

5/6

Check the reduced section early enough to use it. Buy only what you can cook or freeze safely. A discount is not a saving if the food ends up in the bin.

6/6

Try one “leftovers night” each week. Combine odds and ends into fried rice, soup, wraps, omelettes, or a grain bowl. The best grocery deal may already be in your fridge.

Variant 3

1/6

A simple grocery-saving system:

  1. Check the fridge, freezer, and cupboards.
  2. Plan meals around what needs using.
  3. Write the gaps.
  4. Shop only for the gaps.

2/6

Keep a price list for 10 staples you buy often. You’ll quickly learn what a real bargain looks like—and avoid buying something just because the sign says “deal.”

3/6

Use meat as an ingredient, not always the centre of the plate. Tacos, curries, soups, noodles, and pasta can use smaller amounts while beans, eggs, or vegetables add bulk.

4/6

Choose sturdy produce when your week is busy: carrots, cabbage, apples, onions, potatoes, and frozen veg. Delicate items are great too—if you have a plan to eat them quickly.

5/6

Cook once, eat twice. Double a sauce, soup, curry, or grain and refrigerate or freeze half. Future-you gets a cheap meal and skips an expensive takeaway.

6/6

Review the receipt after shopping. Circle the items you didn’t plan to buy. Don’t feel guilty—just look for the pattern and make one adjustment next week.

Variant 4

1/6

Before your next grocery trip, take a 10-minute inventory. Check expiry dates, open packets, and the back of the freezer. Your shopping list should begin with what you already own.

2/6

Pick two low-cost “base meals” for the week: soup, rice bowls, baked potatoes, pasta, or bean chilli. Add different toppings or sides so they don’t feel repetitive.

3/6

Skip pre-cut and individually packaged versions when convenient. Whole vegetables, a block of cheese, and a larger tub of yoghurt often cost less per serving.

4/6

Store food to make it last: freeze bread, keep herbs in water, use airtight containers, and separate bananas from other fruit. A few minutes of storage can save a full replacement purchase.

5/6

Watch for subscription-style deals and multi-buys. They’re only bargains if you need the quantity and can use it before it spoils. “Two for one” is not a reason to buy two.

6/6

Give yourself a small treat budget. A planned snack is easier to manage than trying to be perfect, then impulse-buying half the aisle after a tiring day.

Variant 5

1/6

The cheapest grocery trip starts at home. Photograph your fridge and pantry before leaving. Use the pictures to build your list and avoid buying duplicates hiding behind everything else.

2/6

Make a “flexible list”: must-buy items, nice-to-have items, and substitutes. If the chicken is expensive, you already know whether eggs, beans, or lentils can fill the role.

3/6

Compare shops for your regular basket, not just one bargain item. Travel costs and impulse purchases can erase savings. Convenience is part of the price too.

4/6

Batch-prep ingredients, not just finished meals. Cook a pot of grains, wash vegetables, or make a sauce. Mix and match through the week before anything gets forgotten.

5/6

Freeze food in useful portions and label it with the date. Mystery containers get ignored; clearly marked portions become easy lunches, dinners, or emergency meals.

6/6

At the end of the week, note one win and one leak. Maybe you saved with frozen veg—or overspent on drinks. Keep the win, fix the leak, and repeat next week.
