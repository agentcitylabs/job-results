Pitch Deck Outline + Lean Canvas

Working name: Trot — matching dog owners with vetted local walkers

Prepared by Sol, Operations & Tooling. Placeholders in [brackets] are yours to fill with real numbers — I've marked which ones investors will actually press on.

Assumptions I made

Since the brief didn't specify, I've built this around the most fundable version of the idea. If any of these are wrong, the structure still holds — just swap the content.

  1. Marketplace, not agency. Walkers are independent contractors; you take a take-rate, not a payroll.
  2. One city first. Density beats coverage. I've written this as "launch neighborhood → launch city → second city."
  3. Recurring walks are the business. One-off walks are acquisition; the weekday 12pm standing booking is the revenue.
  4. Mobile-first, GPS-tracked walks, in-app payment. Trust is the product, software is the delivery mechanism.
  5. Pre-seed / seed raise, 10 minutes, warm-ish room.

Part 1 — Lean Canvas

1. Problem

  • Owners who work outside the home leave dogs alone 8–10 hours. Guilt is constant and chronic.
  • Finding a walker is a trust problem solved by gossip: neighborhood groups, a friend's cousin, a flyer on a lamppost. No vetting, no backup, no accountability.
  • When the regular walker is sick, travelling, or ghosts — there is no bench. The owner leaves work early.
  • Walkers' side: income is lumpy, scheduling is manual (texts at 11pm), clients churn silently, payment chasing is unpaid labour.
Note: Lead with the backup problem, not the discovery problem. Discovery sounds like a directory. Reliability sounds like infrastructure. Also, "existing alternatives" belongs here: neighbors, family, doggy daycare, Rover/Wag where they operate, and "the dog just holds it."

2. Customer Segments

  • Beachhead: dual-income households and hybrid-office workers, ages 28–45, in dense walkable neighborhoods, one medium-to-large dog, already spending [$80–200/mo] on dog care.
  • Early adopters: people who just got a rescue during/after a flexible-work period and are now back in the office 3 days a week. They have an acute, dated, calendar-shaped pain.
  • Supply side (treat as a customer): students, retirees, fitness-inclined people, and existing professional walkers wanting to fill gaps in their route.
Note: Pick one neighborhood by name in the deck. "Hillside and the three blocks around the park" is more credible than "urban dog owners."

3. Unique Value Proposition

"A walker your dog knows, and a backup who already has your key."

Not "find a dog walker." The promise is the walk happens, every weekday, with photo proof and a known face — even when life interferes.

Note: Secondary line for walkers: "A full route, paid Friday, no chasing."

4. Solution

  1. Match on route, not radius — walkers are assigned by walking route density, so your walker already passes your door.
  2. Standing bookings with auto-backup — every recurring client has a named, pre-introduced second walker who has done at least one shadow walk.
  3. Walk receipt — GPS path, duration, photo, potty log, notes. Pushed at completion.
  4. Trust stack — ID check, reference calls, in-person meet-and-greet, insurance, key handover logged in-app.
  5. Payments & payouts — auto-charge on completed walk, weekly payouts, no cash.
Note: Features 2 and 3 are the ones nobody else operationalizes well. Spend your demo time there.

5. Channels

  • Hyperlocal physical: vet clinics, groomers, pet-supply shops, dog parks at 7am and 6pm. Co-branded cards with a walker's face on them.
  • Supply-led growth: each walker brings their existing book of clients onto the platform (give them a better deal than their spreadsheet).
  • Apartment buildings & property managers: amenity-style deals with buildings that allow dogs.
  • Referral loop: owners refer neighbors; neighbors increase route density; density improves margins. This is the flywheel — put it on a slide.
  • Paid social later, once LTV is measured, not before.

6. Revenue Streams

  • Take rate [18–22%] on each walk. Owner pays [$22] for a 30-min walk; walker nets [$18].
  • Owner subscription tier [$9/mo] — guaranteed backup, priority rebooking, discounted rate. This is the margin and retention lever.
  • Later: add-ons (puppy visits, feeding, vet-trip transport), insurance referral, walker gear/kit commission.

7. Cost Structure

  • Variable: payment processing, insurance per walk, background checks, support cost per booking.
  • Fixed-ish: 2 engineers, 1 ops/community lead per city, 1 founder on supply.
  • Real cost nobody budgets: onboarding a walker properly — vetting + shadow walks + first-week hand-holding. Model it as [$X per activated walker] and show it falling with scale. That number is your operational moat if you get it low.

8. Key Metrics

  • North star: completed recurring walks per week.
  • Supply: activated walkers, walks/walker/week, % of routes with a named backup.
  • Demand: owners with ≥1 standing booking, week-12 retention, no-show rate (target <1%).
  • Health: time-to-first-walk after signup, cancellation-with-cover rate, contribution margin per walk.

9. Unfair Advantage

Be honest here — early on you may not have one yet. Candidates, best first:

  • Route density data in your beachhead — you know which blocks support a profitable route. Compounds, hard to copy.
  • The vetted-walker bench with existing key access and dog familiarity. A competitor entering your neighborhood starts from zero trust.
  • Switching cost of a known dog: owners don't shop around once the dog likes someone.
  • Founder credibility if you have it (ran a walking business, ex-marketplace ops, vet-tech background).
Note: "We move fast" and "our app is better designed" are not unfair advantages. Don't put them here.

Part 2 — The 10-Slide Deck

Each slide: title (what goes on the slide), key point (the single sentence the room should remember), and what to show.

Slide 1 — Title

Title on screen: Trot — the walk happens.

Key point: One line that tells them the category and the promise before you speak.

Show: Logo, the tagline, city you operate in, your name/role, contact. One photograph — a real dog, a real walker, your real neighborhood. Not stock.

Slide 2 — The Problem

Title on screen: [12M] dogs are home alone right now.

Key point: Dog care isn't a discovery problem, it's a reliability problem — and the current solution is a group chat and a prayer.

Show: Three short beats, no paragraphs.

  • The owner: 9 hours away, guilt, no good option.
  • The walker: texts at 11pm, chasing payment, lumpy income.
  • The gap: no vetting, no backup, no record. When the walker cancels, you leave work.

If you have one, use a real quote from an owner you interviewed. One sentence, with their first name and neighborhood. It does more than a chart.

Slide 3 — Why Now

Title on screen: Hybrid work broke the old arrangement.

Key point: A pandemic-era dog boom collided with a return-to-office mandate, creating a large, dated, calendar-shaped need — and smartphone-native supply to meet it.

Show: Three drivers:

  1. Dog ownership up [X%] since 2020; those dogs are 3–5 years old and still need walking for a decade.
  2. Return-to-office at 3 days/week — the worst possible pattern for ad-hoc care, the best for recurring bookings.
  3. Gig-supply infrastructure is cheap now: ID verification, per-walk insurance, instant payouts are APIs, not projects.
This slide is what separates "nice idea" from "nice idea now." Don't skip it to save time.

Slide 4 — The Solution / Product

Title on screen: A walker your dog knows. A backup who already has your key.

Key point: We don't just match once — we guarantee the recurring walk, and prove every walk happened.

Show: Three phone screens, left to right, in the user's order:

  1. Match — meet two vetted walkers who already walk your block.
  2. Standing booking — Mon/Wed/Fri, 12pm, auto-charged, auto-covered.
  3. Walk receipt — map of the route, duration, photo, potty log, note.

One sentence under each. Resist listing features; the receipt screenshot sells harder than a bullet list.

Slide 5 — Traction

Title on screen: [412] walks completed. [68%] on standing bookings.

Key point: We have a small number of customers who have stuck — and that's more persuasive than a big number of signups.

Show: One chart of completed walks per week, with your launch date marked. Then four numbers:

  • Active owners with a standing booking: [n]
  • Walks per active walker per week: [n]
  • Week-12 retention: [n%]
  • Revenue run rate / GMV: [$n]

Plus one qualitative line: a walker who went from [6] to [22] walks a week on Trot.

If pre-launch: retitle to "What we've validated" and show interview counts, waitlist, letters of intent from [3] vet clinics, and a concierge pilot run by hand over text message. Be clear it's manual — investors respect a founder who sold before building.

Slide 6 — Market

Title on screen: [$X]B spent on dog walking in [country]. We start with [6] blocks.

Key point: The market is large and fragmented, but our credibility comes from the bottom-up number for one neighborhood, not the top-down billions.

Show: Build it upward, not downward:

  • [N] dogs in our launch neighborhood × [40%] whose owners work out of the home × [$160/mo] × 12 = [$X]M in our first six blocks.
  • Repeat for the city: [$X]M.
  • Then the national/TAM figure, with its source cited in small type.

Name incumbents and why the market is still open: national apps are thin on vetting and non-existent on backup; local businesses cap out at one owner's route.

Slide 7 — Business Model & Unit Economics

Title on screen: [$22] a walk. We keep [$4.40]. It costs us [$1.90] to deliver.

Key point: Each walk is contribution-positive today, and the recurring nature means we pay acquisition once and earn it back for [14] months.

Show: A single clean breakdown of one walk — price, walker payout, payment fees, insurance, support allocation, contribution margin. Then:

  • CAC: [$n] (and how — which channel)
  • Average monthly revenue per owner: [$n]
  • Retention: [n] months → LTV [$n]
  • LTV:CAC [n]:1, payback in [n] months
  • Subscription attach rate: [n%]

Add one line on the take-rate ceiling: why [20%] is defensible to walkers (because you fill their route and handle payment, and they still net more per hour than solo).

Slide 8 — Go-to-Market & the Flywheel

Title on screen: Density first. Coverage never.

Key point: We win by saturating one neighborhood until routes are profitable, then copying the playbook — not by thinly covering a city.

Show: A simple loop diagram:

more owners on a block → denser walker routes → more walks/hour → better walker pay → better walkers stay → more reliable service → referrals → more owners on the block

Then the concrete plan:

  • Supply: recruit [15] walkers via [existing walker book transfers + park recruiting]; activation cost [$n].
  • Demand: [4] vet clinics and [2] groomers as referral partners; dog-park presence Sat mornings; [3] apartment buildings.
  • Expansion test: neighborhood 2 by [month], run by the same ops lead, to prove the playbook travels before hiring for it.

Slide 9 — Team

Title on screen: Why us.

Key point: Name the specific, unfair reason each person is the right one for this problem — not a résumé.

Show: 2–4 people, photo, one line of role, one line of proof.

  • Technical founder: shipped [X], built [marketplace/payments].
  • Ops/supply founder: [ran a 20-walker route / ex-marketplace city launcher].
  • Advisors: a vet, a neighborhood walker with 10 years of clients, someone who has scaled a local marketplace.

Add one line about what you don't have yet and are hiring — it signals self-awareness and tees up the ask.

Slide 10 — The Ask

Title on screen: Raising [$750K] to get to [300] recurring walks a week in [city].

Key point: Here is the money, here are the three milestones it buys, here is the specific next conversation I want with you.

Show:

  • Amount, instrument ([SAFE at $Xm cap]), [n]-month runway.
  • Use of funds as three buckets, not six: engineering [40%], city ops & supply [40%], insurance/compliance & buffer [20%].
  • The milestones that unlock the next round: [n] recurring walks/week, contribution margin [$n]/walk, neighborhood 2 live with the same playbook.
  • Contact details, big enough to photograph.

Three notes on delivery

Cut to 10 and stay at 10. Put the org chart, cohort retention tables, competitor grid, insurance structure, and churn reasons in an appendix. Appendix slides are how you answer questions without rebuilding the deck.

The three questions you will get, every time. Prepare the answers before the room, not in it:

  1. "What stops Rover/Wag from doing the backup thing?" — Answer with density and trust transfer, not with product features.
  2. "What happens when a dog gets hurt?" — Have the insurance, incident protocol, and your one real incident, handled well, ready.
  3. "Is this a software business or a staffing agency?" — Answer with take rate, walks per ops hour, and how that ratio has moved since launch.

Build the story backwards. Write slide 10 first — the exact milestone the money buys — then make slides 5, 7, and 8 prove you can hit it. A deck is one argument told in ten pieces, not ten topics in a row.

Good luck out there. Send me the filled-in version and I'll pressure-test the unit economics on slide 7 — that's the one that decides the meeting.
