# Casual Puzzle Game: First-Week Launch Plan

**Assumption:** This is a free-to-play iOS and Android game with short levels, a small launch budget, and basic analytics in place. The first launch is in one English-speaking market.

**Launch goal:** Find players who enjoy the core puzzle, get them to a satisfying first win, and learn whether they return the next day. Scale promotion only after that experience works.

## Audience

**Primary audience:** Adults aged 25–44 who already enjoy casual puzzle games and want a quick break during a commute, between tasks, or on the sofa.

**What they’re looking for:** A puzzle that is easy to understand, satisfying to finish, and challenging without feeling unfair.

**Launch message:** “A small puzzle. A satisfying win. Your next five-minute break.”

Show real gameplay in every promotion: one clear problem, the player’s move, and the payoff. Avoid implying mechanics or rewards that aren’t in the game.

## Three Launch Channels

| Channel | What we’ll publish | What we’ll measure |
|---|---|---|
| **1. App Store and Google Play listings** | Lead with the clearest gameplay screenshot. Use the next two screenshots to show progression and variety. Add a short gameplay preview if ready. Keep the description focused on the five-minute-break promise. | Product-page visits, visit-to-install conversion, and installs by store. These show whether the listing turns interest into downloads. |
| **2. TikTok organic** | Publish one vertical gameplay clip daily. Rotate three hooks: “What’s your next move?”, a tricky near-fail, and a satisfying solve. End with one simple invitation to play through the profile link. | Views, completion rate, profile-link clicks, and attributed installs where available. Compare hooks; don’t choose a winner on views alone. |
| **3. Small puzzle-game creators** | Contact 15 relevant creators with engaged audiences, not just large follower counts. Offer early access and a compact kit: gameplay footage, three factual talking points, and a tracked link. Aim for 3–5 launch-week posts, subject to replies and budget. Disclose any paid or gifted relationship. | Replies, confirmed posts, tracked clicks, installs where attribution is available, and next-day return rate by source. |

**Budget rule:** Prioritize the listing and organic clips. If funds are available, test a few small creator placements rather than broad paid acquisition. Treat creator pricing and participation as unconfirmed until agreed.

## First-Week Actions

| Day | Action | Check |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Launch cleanly** | Verify both store listings, download links, and analytics. Test a fresh install through the first three levels. Publish the first gameplay clip and send creator outreach. | Installs appear in reporting; tutorial and level events fire correctly; no launch-blocking bugs. |
| **2 — Remove friction** | Review where new players leave. Read every early review and support message. Fix crashes, confusing instructions, or progression blockers before adding reach. Publish a “your next move?” clip. | Tutorial completion, first-level completion, and repeated complaints. |
| **3 — Test the promise** | Publish a satisfying-solve clip. Follow up with interested creators and confirm posting dates. Prepare a revised store screenshot if players misunderstand the game. | Which hook produces clicks? Does player feedback match the store promise? |
| **4 — Inspect the first cohorts** | Check next-day return rates for Days 1–2. Separate sources where sample size allows. Publish a near-fail clip and answer comments. | Are players returning, and does any source bring noticeably better-fit players? |
| **5 — Repeat what works** | Make a new clip using the strongest hook. Share live creator posts with permission. Ship only low-risk fixes; avoid changing difficulty, pricing, and onboarding all at once. | Clicks and installs from the repeat hook; impact of fixes. |
| **6 — Listen for patterns** | Group feedback into bugs, confusion, difficulty, and requests. Publish a gameplay tip that addresses the most common sticking point without spoiling the game. | Fewer repeated complaints; completion of the troublesome level. |
| **7 — Decide next week** | Summarize the funnel, costs, feedback, and fixes. Choose one acquisition experiment and one player-experience improvement for week two. | Continue, revise, or pause each channel based on evidence—not launch-day excitement. |

## Measurement and Decision Rules

Before launch, record **first open, tutorial start/completion, level start/completion, and session start**, using privacy-appropriate analytics. Give each creator a unique tracked link; record attribution gaps rather than guessing.

Use these as **provisional internal targets, not industry guarantees**:

- **Tutorial completion:** At least 80% of tutorial starters finish.
- **First-level completion:** At least 85% of first-level starters finish.
- **Day-1 retention:** At least 25% of new players return the next calendar day.
- **Paid creator efficiency:** Cost per attributed install, plus the quality of those players’ return rate.

Review results daily, but wait for roughly **100 new players with a complete next-day observation window** before treating retention as more than directional. If reach is low, keep testing distribution. If installs arrive but onboarding or retention misses the targets, improve the game experience before spending more. **Week-one success is a reliable learning loop—not a large download count that disappears overnight.**
